Chapter 1 · Lesson 16

Three Types of Muscle Tissue: Location, Control, Function

Before you can document any muscular finding, you need to know which type of muscle you're dealing with — because the clinical implications differ significantly. The three muscle types are distinguished by their location, appearance, and the type of nervous system control they operate under.

Skeletal Muscle — The Voluntary Mover

Skeletal musclemy/o (muscle) + muscul/oVoluntary, striated muscle attached to bone via tendons. Controls all intentional movement. ~40% of body weight. is what most people think of when they hear "muscle." It is voluntary (you consciously control it), striated (has visible bands under microscopy), and attached to bone via tendons. It controls every intentional movement — walking, lifting, breathing, speaking. In the clinical setting, you will assess skeletal muscle function through strength testing (graded 0–5), ROM assessment, and observation of gait. Key conditions: myalgiamy/o (muscle) + -algia (pain)Muscle pain. Can be localized (overuse) or diffuse (viral infection, statin medication side effect)., myopathymy/o (muscle) + -pathy (disease)Disease of muscle tissue. Can be genetic (muscular dystrophy) or acquired (inflammatory myopathy)., atrophya- (without) + troph/o (nourishment)Decrease in muscle size and strength from disuse, immobilization, or denervation. Measurable as circumference difference., and hypertrophyhyper- (excessive) + troph/o (growth/nourishment)Increase in muscle fiber size from repeated resistance training. Not pathological — an adaptive response..

Smooth Muscle — The Involuntary System

Smooth muscleleiomy/o (smooth muscle)Involuntary, non-striated muscle in walls of hollow organs — blood vessels, GI tract, bladder, uterus. lines the walls of hollow organs — blood vessels, the GI tract, bladder, and uterus. It is involuntary (controlled by the autonomic nervous system) and non-striated (no visible bands). You cannot consciously control it, but drugs can: antispasmodics relax smooth muscle in the GI tract, vasodilators relax smooth muscle in blood vessel walls. Clinically relevant conditions: smooth muscle spasmGreek: spasmos (convulsion)Involuntary sudden muscle contraction. In hollow organs: colic, ureteral spasm. In skeletal muscle: overuse or electrolyte imbalance. causes colic (intestinal) or ureteral spasm (kidney stone pain).

Cardiac Muscle — The Autorhythmic Heart

Cardiac muscle is found exclusively in the heart wall (myocardium). Like skeletal muscle, it is striated. Like smooth muscle, it is involuntary. What makes it unique: it is autorhythmic — it generates its own electrical impulse (via the SA node) without needing a signal from the brain. It contracts approximately 100,000 times per day without fatigue. After a myocardial infarction (heart attack), cardiac muscle cells that die cannot regenerate — this is why MI damage is permanent, and why early intervention is critical.

Atrophy vs. Hypertrophy: Two Sides of the troph/o Coin

These two conditions share the same root word — troph/o (nourishment/development) — but are clinical opposites. Atrophya- (without) + troph/o (nourishment)Muscle wasting. Decrease in muscle fiber size and strength from disuse, immobilization, denervation, or malnutrition. is muscle wasting — the result of disuse (after casting), denervation (after nerve injury), or systemic disease. You measure it by comparing limb circumference: "Quadriceps atrophy noted — right thigh circumference 2cm less than left." Normal adaptive response — not pathological. is the adaptive increase in muscle fiber size from resistance training. It is not pathological — a competitive athlete's quad hypertrophy is documented as a finding, not a diagnosis.

Eight Movement Terms: The Shared Language of Clinical Documentation

Every physical therapist, sports medicine clinician, orthopedic surgeon, and ER nurse uses these eight terms to describe joint motion. They are not interchangeable. Using the wrong term in a clinical note — "the patient demonstrated abduction when they bent their elbow" — creates documentation errors that affect treatment decisions, insurance authorization, and legal records.

Chapter 3 · Lesson 17 — Goniometer Lab

ROM Assessment: AROM, PROM, and the Goniometer

Range of motion assessment is a core clinical competency for physical therapists, athletic trainers, orthopedic nurses, and sports medicine providers. It quantifies joint function, documents the impact of injury, tracks recovery progress, and provides legal documentation of impairment. The key to accurate ROM documentation is understanding the difference between two types of assessment.

AROM vs. PROM: A Critical Clinical Distinction

AROMActive Range of MotionROM produced by the PATIENT using their own muscle strength. Tests motor function and pain inhibition. (Active Range of Motion) is the movement a patient produces using their own muscular effort — no assistance from the clinician. When a patient can't reach full AROM, it may indicate motor weakness, pain inhibition, or tendon/nerve involvement. PROMPassive Range of MotionROM produced by the CLINICIAN moving the patient's limb. Tests structural joint integrity, not motor function. (Passive Range of Motion) is the movement produced when the clinician moves the patient's limb — the patient is relaxed and contributes no effort. PROM tests structural joint integrity and capsular restriction.

The relationship between AROM and PROM tells you what's wrong: If PROM > AROM, the joint can structurally move (PROM shows this) but the patient can't generate that motion — suggesting motor weakness, pain inhibition, or neuromuscular deficit. If both AROM and PROM are limited equally, the limitation is structural — a tight capsule, scar tissue, or contracture. Document: "AROM right elbow flexion 110°, PROM 125° — gap suggests pain/motor limitation rather than structural block."

Goniometer Technique: Four Steps

A goniometerGreek: gonia (angle) + metr/o (measure)Clinical instrument used to measure joint angle and range of motion in degrees. Two arms connected at a pivot. is a simple hinged instrument with two arms and a degree scale. Proper placement is everything — a misplaced axis produces inaccurate measurements that cannot be compared across clinicians or visits.

  • Axis placement: Center the axis of the goniometer at the center of the joint being measured (lateral knee joint line, lateral elbow epicondyle, lateral malleolus for ankle).
  • Stationary arm: Align with the proximal body segment — the part that does NOT move (femur for knee measurements).
  • Moving arm: Align with the distal body segment — the part that DOES move (tibia/fibula for knee measurements).
  • Record and compare: Note the start position, move through full ROM, record the end position in degrees. Always compare bilaterally — the uninjured limb is your reference.

Documenting a ROM Deficit

A ROM deficitrange of motion + deficit (deficere = to fail)A measurable reduction in joint motion compared to the contralateral limb or established normal values. is always documented with both values: "Patient demonstrates 12° dorsiflexion deficit at right ankle compared to left (8° right vs. 20° left)." Never document a deficit without the reference — "limited dorsiflexion" tells you nothing actionable. The comparison number drives the treatment plan and the insurance authorization.

Task 1 · Interpret the AROM vs. PROM Gap
For dorsiflexion, AROM is 8° and PROM is 14° on the right side. The gap is 6°. What does this gap tell you clinically? What is the most likely explanation for why the patient can move further passively than actively? Use correct terminology from your reading.
When PROM > AROM: the joint can structurally move (PROM demonstrates this) but the patient cannot generate that motion actively. This suggests motor weakness, pain inhibition (the patient stops before the structural limit due to pain), or neuromuscular deficit — NOT a structural block. If both were equally limited, you'd suspect a structural issue (contracture, scar tissue).
Task 2 · Document the ROM Findings
Write the Objective (O) section ROM documentation for Jordan K. using correct clinical documentation format. Include all six motions from the table above, document bilaterally, and calculate and state each deficit explicitly. Use the standard deficit documentation phrase format from your reading.
Format for each: "[Motion] [AROM/PROM]: R [value]°, L [value]° — [deficit]° deficit right compared to left." Always state both values — never just say "limited." The deficit number = L value minus R value. Document AROM and PROM separately where both were measured.
Task 3 · Connect to Clinical Conditions
Jordan reports the heel feels "tight going upstairs" — this is a symptom related to a specific clinical condition you studied this week. Name the condition using the correct medical term, explain what the root words mean, and explain why dorsiflexion is specifically limited in this condition.
Tightness in the heel going upstairs = Achilles region. The Achilles tendon connects the gastrocnemius/soleus (calf muscles) to the calcaneus (heel bone). When it is inflamed or tight, it resists being stretched — which is exactly what happens during dorsiflexion (pulling the foot up toward the shin). Think: tendin/o (tendon) + -itis (inflammation).
Task 4 · Atrophy Assessment + Documentation
After two weeks non-weight-bearing, you observe that Jordan's right calf appears visibly smaller than the left. You measure: right calf circumference 30cm, left 33cm. Using the correct clinical term and root word breakdown, document this finding and explain the physiological reason it occurred after immobilization.
Term: atrophy. a- (without) + troph/o (nourishment). When a muscle is not being used actively (non-weight-bearing), the motor nerve signals that maintain muscle fiber size decrease, protein synthesis slows, and fiber cross-sectional area decreases. This begins within 48–72 hours of immobilization. Document: "Gastrocnemius/soleus muscle atrophy noted right lower extremity — 3cm circumference deficit compared to contralateral limb."
